The naive version of a microservices project is a monolith with network calls between the pieces. The gateway calls identity to check a token, posts calls media to attach a file, posts calls search to update an index. It works until one of the callees is slow or down, and then every caller inherits the failure.

The interesting alternative: services publish facts about what happened, and other services decide what those facts mean for them.

## The five services

A social product split by responsibility rather than by layer: an API gateway as the single entry point, identity for registration and sessions, posts for creation and deletion, media for uploads, and search for full-text queries over posts.

The event core is small. When a post is created, the posts service publishes `post.created`, and the search service indexes it. When a post is deleted, `post.deleted` goes out, the media service deletes the attached files, and search removes its entries.

## What changed in my head

Producers publish and move on. The posts service does not know that search exists, or that media listens for deletions. That ignorance is the feature. Adding a feature that reacts to posts (notifications, say) means adding a subscriber, not editing the posts service and redeploying everything that depends on it.

It also changes failure handling. A consumer that is temporarily down does not block the producer. The event sits in the queue, and the consumer catches up. The cleanup of a deleted post's files is eventually consistent, and that is an acceptable, deliberate trade rather than an accident.

## Caching and the unglamorous parts

Search sits behind Redis, with MongoDB text indexes doing the actual matching. Caching repeat queries is a small addition that changes how the app feels: the second search is instant. It also taught me that cache invalidation deserves first-class attention, not a `ttl` you set and forget.

Two defaults I would keep in any next version: every service carries its own Jest suite, so tests run at the same granularity as deployment, and the whole stack comes up with `docker-compose up --build`, with the RabbitMQ management UI making the event flow observable while you develop.

Observability of the event flow is underrated. Watching messages move through the management UI is how I found the first duplicate-delivery bug, before it ever reached a test.
